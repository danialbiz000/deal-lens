# Case study: Long-Term Capital Management (1998)

## What happened

Long-Term Capital Management was founded in 1994 by John Meriwether (former head of bond
arbitrage at Salomon Brothers), with a partner roster that included Myron Scholes and
Robert Merton — who shared the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for the
Black-Scholes-Merton option pricing framework. The fund ran highly leveraged
**convergence/relative-value arbitrage**: betting that prices of closely related
securities (on-the-run vs. off-the-run Treasuries, sovereign bond spreads, swap spreads,
equity volatility) would converge toward their historically "fair" relationship. Individual
trades had small expected edges, so the fund used enormous leverage — on-balance-sheet
leverage over 25:1, with off-balance-sheet derivative notional exposure exceeding
$1 trillion — to turn small, statistically-reliable-looking edges into a large return.

Russia's default and rouble devaluation in August 1998 triggered a global "flight to
quality": investors around the world simultaneously fled toward the most liquid, safest
assets (on-the-run US Treasuries) and away from everything else, regardless of the
fundamental economic link between the assets being sold. Spreads that LTCM's models
treated as governed by (mostly) independent, mean-reverting relationships instead moved
together, in the same direction, at the same time — and kept widening instead of
converging.

**Timeline, in more detail:**

- **1994–1997**: the fund returns roughly 20-40% annually net of fees, gaining a
  reputation for near-riskless, "picking up nickels" arbitrage — a track record that,
  in hindsight, is itself a textbook setup for overconfidence in the model's tail-risk
  assumptions (the same pattern seen in Amaranth's Brian Hunter, below).
- **August 17, 1998**: Russia defaults on its domestic rouble-denominated debt (GKOs)
  and devalues the rouble, triggering the "flight to quality" described above.
- **August–September 1998**: LTCM loses roughly $1.9 billion in August alone; by
  September 21 the fund's capital had fallen from about $4.7 billion at the start of the
  year to roughly $600 million, against essentially unchanged (or in some cases larger)
  notional derivative exposure — leverage that had been "only" ~25:1 on a calm-regime
  capital base became effectively far higher once the capital base itself collapsed.
- **September 23, 1998**: with LTCM days from a disorderly default that regulators feared
  could force a fire-sale of well over $1 trillion notional of positions across
  Wall Street, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York (not the Fed's own money, notably —
  it organized but did not fund the deal) convened 14 banks to inject $3.625 billion in
  exchange for 90% of the fund's equity, unwinding the book in an orderly way over the
  following months instead.

By the time of the recapitalization, the fund had lost roughly 90% of its capital in about
six weeks — a scale of loss its own risk models had assigned a vanishingly small
probability to over any comparable horizon.

## Why this is a rationality-assumption failure, not just "a bad trade"

LTCM's risk models were built on standard, academically respectable assumptions: that
historical correlations and volatilities were a reasonable guide to near-future risk, and
that markets, composed of rational arbitrageurs, would correct mispricings rather than
extend them. Both assumptions describe how *many* market participants behave *most* of
the time. Neither held during the crisis:

- **Correlations that were low-to-moderate historically moved toward one.** The very
  diversification that made the portfolio look safe on a calm-regime covariance matrix
  evaporated exactly when it was needed, because the *cause* of the losses (indiscriminate
  flight to liquidity) hit every position at once, regardless of each trade's individual
  fundamentals.
- **The "smart money" made things worse, not better.** Classical efficient-market theory
  assumes arbitrageurs correct mispricings. Here, other leveraged players — some of them
  reportedly aware of or guessing at LTCM's positions — were forced (or chose) to
  deleverage into the same illiquid trades LTCM needed to exit, pushing spreads further
  from "fair value" rather than back toward it. Rational-seeming individual deleveraging
  decisions aggregated into a collectively self-reinforcing, panic-driven spiral.
- **Leverage converted a plausible loss into a solvency event.** The underlying spread
  moves were, in absolute terms, not unprecedented — LTCM's own models reportedly assigned
  a very low probability to a loss of this magnitude within a short window, largely
  because the model underweighted how correlated the shocks could become, not because the
  individual spread moves were physically impossible. See
  `../risk_simulation/fat_tails_vs_normal.py` for a Monte Carlo illustration of exactly
  this mechanism (Gaussian, calm-regime VaR vs. a fat-tailed, regime-switching-correlation
  process) — a stylized version of the same failure mode, not a reconstruction of LTCM's
  actual book.

## The behavioral lens

The irrationality here was not primarily LTCM's own — it was in the *rest of the market*:
herding (flight to quality), correlated forced-selling by other leveraged players, and a
collective panic response that a "rational agents, efficient markets" model does not
naturally produce on its own. LTCM's failure is best read as: *a model of rational markets
is only as good as its assumption that other participants will keep behaving rationally
(or at least independently) under stress — and that assumption is precisely the one that
breaks in a genuine crisis.*
